I still remember how I first came to know about the IIT Madras BS in Data Science programme.

It was during the COVID period. My mother saw a newspaper advertisement about an online data science degree being offered by IIT Madras and showed it to me. At first, the entire thing sounded unusual.

A degree where you first write a qualifier after just one month of study material? Online? Flexible? Multiple exits? I had honestly never heard of something structured like that before.

Out of curiosity more than certainty, I enrolled.

And that one decision ended up changing the direction of my life.

## Being part of the first batch

One thing that makes this journey special to me is that I belonged to the very first batch of students enrolled into the programme.

Back then, it was much smaller and much simpler. There was no BS degree yet, no Postgraduate Diploma, and no MS pathway either. It was originally a 3-year BSc programme.

Today, the programme has grown massively — thousands of students, multiple levels, dedicated projects, and more than 9,000 videos on the official YouTube channel. At that time, there were barely 4–5 videos uploaded.

Pilot batch students were basically the guinea pigs for a lot of the subjects and systems. Many things were being tested and improved in real time. Looking back now, it honestly feels cool to have witnessed the programme evolve from its earliest phase into what it is today.

## The qualifier system

The qualifier process itself was one of the smartest parts of the programme design.

Initially, I thought: how can a serious degree have just one month of preparation before entry?

But later I understood the genius behind it.

The qualifier is intentionally approachable if you sincerely pay attention to lectures and practice consistently. It gives students from very different educational backgrounds a fair entry point into the programme. But as you move through the levels, the difficulty ramps up significantly. That is where the rigor truly starts appearing.

The programme does not try to eliminate students immediately. Instead, it slowly transforms them. Subjects become harder, projects become more demanding, and expectations rise naturally with every level.

## The people behind the programme

Because the batch size was smaller in the beginning, we were much closer to the instructors and organizing team.

People like Andrew Sir, Bharathi Ma’am, and of course Madhavan Sir became familiar faces throughout the journey. There was a level of direct interaction that felt very personal compared to how massive the programme is now.

You could genuinely feel how much effort the team was putting into building something entirely new in Indian education.

## Software development no longer felt mysterious

Before joining this programme, software development honestly felt like a mystery to me. I did not know where to begin, what technologies to learn, how people actually built software, or what “real programming” even looked like.

The programme gradually gave structure to that confusion.

Course by course and project by project, things slowly started making sense. Programming stopped feeling like magic and started feeling learnable.

More importantly, the degree exposed me to how broad the field actually is. Data science is not just “data science.” The skills branch into software engineering, AI and machine learning, analytics, backend systems, business intelligence, automation, and research.

What surprised me most was realizing that these skills can be applied almost anywhere — biology, chemistry, finance, healthcare, and so many other fields. Once you learn how to think computationally, you begin seeing applications everywhere.

## The hidden gems: group projects

One thing I think students underestimate in this programme is the value of group projects.

Most BS and diploma-level subjects include collaborative components, and many students initially treat them as optional burdens. I think they are actually hidden gems of the degree.

These projects teach things no lecture can — collaboration, communication, leadership, ownership, conflict resolution, and managing deadlines. Sometimes you carry the team, sometimes the team carries you, and sometimes you learn how to lead properly for the first time.

And because this is a degree programme filled with highly capable and motivated students from across the country, the connections you make can genuinely stay valuable long-term.

I would honestly suggest not skipping these opportunities.

## The team that changed everything

One of the biggest examples of this happened during my Software Engineering project.

A randomly formed team introduced me to two people who still remain among some of my closest friends today. What started as a course project eventually turned into a long-term team, and later we went on to win the Google Exchange AI Hackathon among 38,000+ participants.

## The first Paradox

Another unforgettable memory was the very first Paradox — the fest exclusive to BS students.

Students from all over India came together. It was chaotic, loud, exciting, and genuinely memorable. For many of us, it was the first time meeting classmates, instructors, organizers, and online friends in person.

There were hackathons, workshops, competitions, tech discussions, random conversations that went on for hours, and the general excitement of finally seeing the community beyond a screen.

It made the programme suddenly feel very real.

## Becoming a Teaching Assistant

One of the most rewarding opportunities I got through the programme was becoming a Teaching Assistant for PDSA (Programming, Data Structures and Algorithms).

I ended up working at the IIT Madras campus for around four months, and that experience taught me far more than I expected.

I worked under Atul Sir and Bhasker Sir, both of whom guided me incredibly well throughout the process. I conducted online sessions, solved doubts, interacted with learners, and experienced the scale of the programme firsthand.

What amazed me most was the diversity of students. Seeing working professionals with 20–30 years of industry experience learning enthusiastically alongside 18-year-old students was honestly surreal.

It showed me that learning truly has no age barrier.

## Watching the programme evolve

Sometimes I compare the programme now to what it looked like when we joined.

Things were simpler then. TDM was much easier than what students deal with now — we were lucky there, I guess. There were no separate large-scale projects for subjects like BDM or MAD at the diploma level back then because they were integrated directly into the coursework itself.

The structure today is far more mature and rigorous, and honestly, seeing that evolution firsthand feels special.

We experienced the programme while it was still discovering its own identity.

## Looking back

When I joined, I enrolled mostly out of curiosity.

I did not know how deeply this programme would influence my life.

It gave me technical skills, confidence, direction, friendships, mentors, opportunities, and most importantly, a pathway into software and technology.

The version of me who first saw that newspaper advertisement during COVID would probably never imagine building projects, working as a TA, attending hackathons, winning competitions, collaborating with talented people from across India, or confidently pursuing software engineering and AI.

And honestly, I still feel like I am only getting started.
